Ogden’s Water, Then and Now

From a single well in 1889 to a reservoir built right over the wells to a $100 million pipe being laid now, here’s where Ogden’s drinking water has come from.

Posted Oct. 18, 2026 · Updated Oct. 18, 2026
bzzbzzbzzbzz

The short version

  • As Ogden grew in the early 1900s, it needed more clean water. The city turned to artesian wells in the Ogden Valley, at a place that became known as Artesian Park.
  • About 50 wells were dug from around 1914 into the 1930s. By 1933 they supplied about 40,000 Ogden residents.
  • The federal government built Pineview Dam from 1934 to 1937. Instead of giving up the wells, Ogden had them capped and piped under the new reservoir. Pineview was later enlarged to more than twice its first size.
  • The canyon pipes are now about 90 years old and leak. Ogden is replacing them in a $100 million project, which is why Pineview was lowered in 2026.

Then: Artesian Park

As Ogden grew into a railroad and business center, it needed new sources of clean water. Starting in 1914, wells were dug near where the forks of the Ogden River meet in the Ogden Valley, Utah Humanities reported. A 1929 typhoid outbreak, blamed on contaminated water from Wheeler Creek, sped up the drilling.

The wells flowed on their own. Between September 1933 and October 1934, about 11,750 acre-feet of water came from Artesian Park, according to a 1937 Department of the Interior study, and the wells served about 40,000 Ogden residents.

The park was also a place to visit. A local history from Weber State University describes an ice cream shop where Pineview Dam now stands, and canyon train trips to Artesian Park every 20 minutes for five cents in 1924.

Sources differ on the details. One local history dates the first well to 1889. The Bureau of Reclamation says the city dug fifty wells between 1915 and 1925. Utah Humanities and a 2019 state study both count 51 wells in all.

The dam goes in over the wells

The Ogden River Project was authorized in 1933. Construction on Pineview Dam began on Sept. 29, 1934, with Utah Construction Company of Ogden and Morrison-Knudsen of Boise as contractors and Civilian Conservation Corps crews helping clear the site, according to the Bureau of Reclamation. Storage began in November 1936, and the work was finished in 1937 (the dam’s top wall was completed in 1939).

Ogden did not want to give up its well water. So Reclamation connected pipes to the wells below the reservoir floor, and the water flowed through a 9,000-foot pipe to the city main. Weber State University’s library calls it the only known system with artesian wells covered by a reservoir.

In 1937, Reclamation turned operation of the project over to the Ogden River Water Users Association. The reservoir also supplies water for about 25,000 acres of farmland and helps with flood control.

Bigger dam, new wells

As part of the later Weber Basin Project, Pineview Dam was raised from 103 to 137 feet, and its storage grew from 44,175 to 110,150 acre-feet, the Bureau of Reclamation says. Weber State’s library dates the enlargement to about 20 years after the dam was finished.

Over time, the original artesian wells were capped and abandoned, and the city drilled new wells that flow to its treatment plant, according to Reclamation. Utah Humanities says the old submerged wells stopped feeding Ogden in 1956; another local history says six new, larger wells were dug in 1970. Those new wells, in a well field next to the reservoir, are still in use. The Ogden Valley News reports the well field supplies more than half of Ogden City’s water.

Now: replacing the pipes

The pipes that carry the well water down Ogden Canyon were laid in the 1930s; the original 36-inch and 24-inch lines carry about two-thirds of Ogden’s drinking water, the Standard-Examiner reported in November 2025. The city replaced the 24-inch pipe in 2014. Leak tests found large leaks, and the old line loses about 1 million gallons a day, The Salt Lake Tribune reported.

“Once a pipe hits 80 years to 90 years old, it is due to fail.”Justin Anderson, executive director of Ogden City Public Services, to the Standard-Examiner (Nov. 2025)

The replacement project is about $100 million, paid for with federal EPA loans, the state Drinking Water Revolving Fund and other grants, the Tribune reported. A new 48-inch pipe is going in under the reservoir, which is why Pineview was lowered in 2026; work there must be done by January so the reservoir can refill. The pipeline serves about 120,000 water users.

Reports differ on some numbers. The Tribune describes 6.4 miles of pipe, a 55-year-old line under the reservoir and a 100-year-old canyon pipe; the Standard-Examiner describes just under eight miles from the well field to the 23rd Street reservoir, with the canyon line laid in the 1930s and completion slated for 2028. The Tribune describes Weber Basin Water Conservancy District as operating Pineview Dam; the Bureau of Reclamation lists the Ogden River Water Users Association as the project’s operator.
