Seven Citizens, Then One Director

For 33 years, a seven-member committee decided Utah’s public records appeals. In 2025 the Legislature replaced it with one appointed director. Here’s what changed, and what the first year shows.

Posted Oct. 18, 2026 · Updated Oct. 18, 2026

The short version

  • When a government office in Utah says no to a records request, you can appeal. From 1992 to 2025, those appeals went to the State Records Committee, seven people that included archivists, citizens and a member of the news media.
  • In 2025, S.B. 277 (primary sponsor Sen. Mike McKell) replaced the committee with one director, a lawyer chosen by the governor and confirmed by the Senate, who runs a new Government Records Office.
  • The first version of the bill would have removed the balancing test, the rule that lets a closed record be released when the public interest is at least as strong. The final law kept it.
  • In his first 12 months, director Lonny Pehrson issued 147 rulings, about twice the committee’s pace, and cleared a backlog of more than 100 cases. People who appealed won less often than before.

Then: the committee, 1992 to 2025

Utah’s public records law, the Government Records Access and Management Act (GRAMA), passed in 1991. The State Records Committee was set up under it in 1992. Its seven members included trained archivists, citizens appointed by the governor and a member of the news media, The Salt Lake Tribune reported.

When a state agency or local government denied a request, the person who asked could appeal to the committee. The committee could also apply the balancing test, deciding whether the public interest in a record was strong enough to release it even when the law allowed it to be kept closed. Of more than 200 committee decisions in recent years, only one was overturned in court, the Tribune reported.

Lawmakers had tried to change GRAMA before. In 2011 they passed changes, then repealed them after pushback from Utahns across the political spectrum, KUER reported.

Why lawmakers changed it

The committee was falling behind. The Tribune reported that appeals in 2023 took an average of 156 days from filing to decision, according to a 2024 audit. The Senate also failed to confirm three appointees, leaving the committee without enough members to meet, and hearings were canceled.

“We have a significant increase in appeals,” Sen. McKell told a Senate committee in February 2025. “These appeals are taking longer and longer to move through.”

The 2025 change

S.B. 277 passed on March 6, 2025; the final House vote was 44-23, KUER reported. It replaced the committee with one lawyer experienced in government records, picked by the governor and confirmed by the Senate, who also runs the new Government Records Office.

Changes made during the session:

  • The balancing test stayed. The first version would have removed it, which Utah’s major news outlets publicly opposed.
  • The director can be removed only “for cause.”
  • An independent performance review of the position was added.

Public comment on the bill was nearly all opposed, KUER reported. Some people objected to putting appeals in one person’s hands; others valued having several viewpoints on the committee. The Utah Media Coalition first opposed the bill, then moved to neutral.

Now: one director

In June 2025 the Utah Senate voted 18-8 to confirm Lonny Pehrson, Gov. Spencer Cox’s pick, as the first director. He had been records counsel for the Utah Attorney General’s Office since 2016, Utah News Dispatch reported. Some senators raised concerns about choosing someone from the AG’s office; Pehrson said he didn’t believe that work would affect how he led the new office.

Under the new law, appeals must be heard within 64 days and decided within 73 days. The office also has a records ombudsman, Monica Minaya, who helps settle disputes before a hearing; she has held 64 mediation meetings, the Tribune reported in August 2026.

After one year, the Tribune found:

  • Pehrson cleared a backlog of more than 100 cases and has received 372 appeals since the office opened.
  • He issued 147 rulings in his first 12 months, about double the committee’s pace.
  • People who appealed won fully or partly about one-third of the time, compared with 40% in the committee’s last three years. The news media won about one in five appeals, compared with more than half before; the Tribune notes that is a small group of cases.

What people say about it

“I feel really good about the outcome.”Sen. Mike McKell, the bill’s primary sponsor, to The Salt Lake Tribune (Aug. 2026)
“I think as you get higher quantity, you tend to get a little less quality.”Lonny Pehrson, government records director, to The Salt Lake Tribune (Aug. 2026)
“It’s our window into government and it’s the ability to participate in government.”Jeff Hunt, attorney who helped write GRAMA in 1991, to The Salt Lake Tribune (Aug. 2026). Hunt said expanding the committee could have sped things up while keeping citizen input.

A reporter for the Utah Investigative Journalism Project told the Tribune she lost both of her appeals before Pehrson, but added, “I’m not waiting six months to get my appeal heard anymore.” A frequent citizen appellant who opposed Pehrson’s confirmation told the Tribune he now considers him “as fair as he can be.”
