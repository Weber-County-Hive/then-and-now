How MIDA Grew

The Military Installation Development Authority was created in 2007 to help keep Hill Air Force Base open. Here’s how it went from one site on the base to eight project areas, a hotel and its own taxes.

Posted Oct. 18, 2026 · Updated Oct. 18, 2026
bzzbzzbzzbzz

The short version

  • MIDA grew out of the fight to keep Utah’s bases open during federal base-closure rounds in the 1990s. The Legislature created it in 2007.
  • For about its first ten years, MIDA had two projects: Falcon Hill, a business park on Hill Air Force Base land, and a small area at Deer Valley, started around 2012 to replace discounted military hotel rooms.
  • From 2019 to 2025, a series of bills, each with Sen. Jerry Stevenson, MIDA’s vice chair, as primary sponsor, added districts that can tax, longer tax increment, and an accommodations tax of up to 15%.
  • Today MIDA lists eight project areas, owns the hotel space at the Grand Hyatt Deer Valley, and has proposed spending $121.6 million next year. Transparent Utah says the state auditor does not audit MIDA; a private firm does its yearly audit.

Then: saving the bases

MIDA’s roots go back to the federal Base Realignment and Closure (BRAC) rounds. The 1993 round closed the Tooele Army Depot, and in 1995 Hill Air Force Base and Defense Depot Ogden were under threat, according to Transparent Utah. Local groups formed to protect the bases and later merged into the Utah Defense Alliance.

“The Utah State Legislature passed the bill creating MIDA in 2007,” Transparent Utah says. Its first board had five members, appointed by the governor, the Senate president and the House speaker. Its first executive director was Rick Mayfield, who had led the Utah Defense Alliance; Paul Morris then led MIDA from 2008 until 2026.

Its first project was Falcon Hill Aerospace Research Park, about 525 acres on Hill Air Force Base land, developed with a private partner.

The Deer Valley hotel

Around 2012, MIDA created a second project area at Deer Valley, about 40 acres, to replace discounted military hotel rooms lost in a land swap for the 2002 Olympics. Over about thirteen years and eight amendments, that area grew to more than 5,000 acres.

A 2019 law, S.B. 269, says hotels and hotel or commercial condo units that MIDA owns pay no property tax or privilege tax, even when a private company runs them. In 2020 the developer donated the hotel site to MIDA; in 2021 MIDA approved up to $260 million in bonds, including about $115 million for the hotel. The Grand Hyatt Deer Valley opened on Nov. 20, 2024. Wasatch County lists the MIDA-owned hotel space at a $0 value. The military benefit today is about 100 discounted hotel rooms.

How the powers grew

From 2019 to 2025, a run of bills widened what MIDA could do. Each had the same primary sponsor, Sen. Jerry Stevenson, who has served on MIDA’s board since 2008 and is its vice chair.

  • S.B. 228 (2019) and S.B. 179 (2020) set up public infrastructure districts (PIDs) inside MIDA areas.
  • S.B. 192 (2020) extended tax increment 15 years at Deer Valley.
  • S.B. 233 (2021) gave those districts direct property-tax authority, with exceptions to some conflict-of-interest, open-meetings and records rules.
  • S.B. 232 (2022) followed.
  • S.B. 169 (2024) allowed an accommodations tax of up to 15% inside project areas, with no end date.
  • S.B. 316 (2025) let MIDA review its own environmental impact and spend project money outside the area that raised it.

One bill went the other way: S.B. 337 (2025) would have folded MIDA into a new coordinating agency. It died without a floor vote. Gov. Spencer Cox later said that bill had been his own reform push.

Now: eight project areas

MIDA’s website lists eight project areas: Falcon Hill (Davis and Weber), Military Recreation (Deer Valley and Jordanelle, Wasatch), Mountain Veterans Program (Sundance, Utah County), Utah National Guard and Utah Data Center (Camp Williams), Dugway Proving Ground and Tooele Army Depot (Tooele), and Stratos (Box Elder).

The two newest came four months apart. On Dec. 16, 2025, MIDA adopted the Tooele Army Depot plan, 43,228 acres. On April 24, 2026, its board unanimously approved Stratos, about 40,000 acres of private land plus 1,279 acres of military and state land, for a data center and energy project in Box Elder County.
